The Lhasa Apso is an ancient breed from Tibet, named after the holy city of Lhasa. For centuries, Lhasas were kept in Buddhist monasteries and the homes of nobles as indoor watchdogs, giving a sharp warning bark when strangers approached. The large Tibetan Mastiffs guarded outside. Lhasas were rarely sold, but were given as gifts, sometimes by the Dalai Lama. Their name is often translated as "long-haired Lhasa dog". Today the Lhasa is a loyal, confident and independent companion.

### What a Lhasa's size means at home

The AKC standard gives about 10–11 in (25.5–28 cm) at the shoulder for males, with females slightly smaller. Lhasas weigh about 5.5–8 kg (12–18 lb). A typical Lhasa reaches mid-shin on an adult. It is sturdier than it looks under its long coat. It suits flats and small homes and usually needs a 24″ (61 cm) crate. Check with the [crate size calculator](/calculators/crate-size/?breed=lhasa-apso).

### Can a Lhasa Apso fly in the cabin?

Sometimes. A light Lhasa in a light carrier may stay within the 8 kg (17.6 lb) limit many airlines set for dog and carrier, but heavier ones won't. Use the [airline cabin check](/calculators/airline-cabin/?breed=lhasa-apso).

### Lhasa Apso vs Shih Tzu and Bichon

The Lhasa is often confused with the [Shih Tzu](/compare/shih-tzu-vs-lhasa-apso/). The Lhasa is a little taller, with a longer nose, a heavier coat and a more independent, watchful nature. The Shih Tzu has a flatter face and is often more outgoing. The [Bichon Frise](/compare/bichon-frise-vs-lhasa-apso/) is about the same size but has a curly white coat and a cheerier, more sociable temperament.

### Coat care

The Lhasa's long, heavy, straight coat reaches the ground if left to grow. A full coat needs brushing every day, right down to the skin, to prevent mats. Most pet owners keep it in a shorter "puppy cut" and visit a groomer every 6–8 weeks. The hair over the eyes is often tied up or trimmed.

### Temperament and health

Lhasas are devoted to their families but often wary of strangers, and they make good watchdogs. They can be independent and stubborn, so patient, consistent training works best. They don't need a lot of exercise. Known health problems include eye conditions (dry eye, progressive retinal atrophy), kidney disease and loose kneecaps. Lhasas are long-lived, often 12–15 years or more.
