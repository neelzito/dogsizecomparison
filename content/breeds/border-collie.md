The Border Collie comes from the hill country along the border between England and Scotland, where shepherds bred their dogs for one thing: working sheep. Looks didn't matter, only ability. Most of today's Border Collies trace back to a dog called Old Hemp, born in Northumberland in 1893 and known for his quiet, intense style of herding. The International Sheep Dog Society, founded in 1906, still registers working Border Collies on the basis of herding trials. The breed is widely regarded as the most intelligent of all dogs, and it is the star of sheepdog trials and dog sports such as agility and flyball.

### What a Border Collie's size means at home

A Border Collie is a medium-sized dog: males stand about 48–56 cm at the shoulder, roughly an adult's knee, and both sexes weigh about 14–25 kg (30–55 lb). It is light and agile rather than heavy, built to run and turn all day on rough hills. Its size suits a medium-sized home, but its energy doesn't suit a small flat with no outdoor space. The [crate size calculator](/calculators/crate-size/?breed=border-collie) suggests a 42″ (107 cm) crate for an average adult. A Border Collie is too heavy for an airline cabin.

### Working lines and show lines

Border Collies vary a lot in looks, because working dogs were never bred to a standard appearance. They can be smooth- or rough-coated, with ears pricked or folded, and in black and white, red, merle, tricolour and more. Show-bred Border Collies tend to be more uniform, with more coat. Working-bred dogs are often leaner and slightly smaller, with even more drive.

### Border Collie vs Australian Shepherd and Rough Collie

People often confuse these herding breeds. The [Australian Shepherd](/compare/border-collie-vs-australian-shepherd/) is a little taller and stockier, often with a bobtail. The [Rough Collie](/compare/rough-collie-vs-border-collie/) (the "Lassie" dog) is bigger, with a long, narrow head and a much fuller coat.

### Not a dog for everyone

A Border Collie needs a lot of exercise and, above all, a job for its brain: training, herding, agility, tricks or long, varied walks. Without it, many become anxious or obsessive, chasing shadows, cars, bikes or even children. The herding instinct can show up as staring, crouching and nipping at heels. In the right home, with active owners who enjoy training, it is a superb, devoted companion.
