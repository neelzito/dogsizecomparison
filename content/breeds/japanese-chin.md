The Japanese Chin is an ancient toy breed that was kept for centuries by Japanese nobility as a lapdog and gift. Its ancestors probably came to Japan from China or Korea. The breed reached Europe and America in the 19th century, after Japan opened to the West, and Queen Alexandra of Britain was a famous owner. With its large eyes, feathered coat and graceful movement, the Chin is often described as cat-like: it climbs, perches on furniture, washes its face with its paws and sometimes spins in circles when excited (the "Chin spin").

### What a Chin's size means at home

The AKC standard calls for 8–11 in (20.5–28 cm) at the shoulder. Chins weigh about 3–5 kg (7–11 lb). A Chin reaches around mid-shin on an adult. It's light, square and fine-boned. It suits any home and usually needs a 24″ (61 cm) crate. Check with the [crate size calculator](/calculators/crate-size/?breed=japanese-chin).

### Flying with a Chin

A Chin is a good size for cabin travel, staying well under the 8 kg (17.6 lb) limit many airlines set, even with a carrier. Check with the [airline cabin check](/calculators/airline-cabin/?breed=japanese-chin). Because of its flat face, many airlines won't accept it in the hold anyway.

### Chin vs Papillon, Brussels Griffon and Silky Terrier

The [Papillon](/compare/japanese-chin-vs-papillon/) is about the same size but has a longer muzzle and butterfly ears. The [Brussels Griffon](/compare/brussels-griffon-vs-japanese-chin/) and the [Affenpinscher](/compare/affenpinscher-vs-japanese-chin/) are similar in size, with wiry coats and monkey-like faces. The [Silky Terrier](/compare/japanese-chin-vs-silky-terrier/) is about the same height, with a terrier temperament.

### Temperament

Chins are affectionate, gentle and dignified, devoted to their family and often reserved with strangers. They're quiet, rarely bark, and are happy with short walks and indoor play. They're sensitive and don't respond well to harsh handling. They suit calm homes, and they're great companions for older people.

### Coat and health

The long, silky single coat sheds moderately and needs brushing a couple of times a week. As a flat-faced breed, the Chin is sensitive to heat and can have breathing problems. Its large eyes are easily injured. Known health problems also include loose kneecaps, heart murmurs, cataracts and GM2 gangliosidosis, a rare inherited nerve disease for which a DNA test exists. Chins often live 10–12 years.
