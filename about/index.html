About the data

Where the numbers come from, how they're processed, and what they can't tell you.

Sources

Heights and weights for {{breed_count}} breeds come from published breed standards. The American Kennel Club (AKC) is the primary source. For breeds the AKC doesn't recognise we use the FCI (Fédération Cynologique Internationale), The Kennel Club (UK), the United Kennel Club (UKC) or the national breed club. Designer crosses such as the Labradoodle have no standard; their ranges are typical values published by breed associations and breeders.

Life expectancy figures are the typical ranges given by kennel clubs and breed clubs. They are averages, not predictions for an individual dog.

How the figures are processed

  • Height is always measured at the withers (top of the shoulder), in centimetres. Inches are converted at 2.54 cm per inch.
  • Ranges: where a standard gives one figure ("27 inches") the range shows that single value. Where it gives an ideal with a tolerance, we show the tolerance range.
  • Male and female: where a standard gives one range for both sexes, both show the same figures.
  • Missing weights: many FCI standards give no weight. We then use the breed club's typical weight range.

Size classes

Each breed is classed by its average adult weight, males and females together: toy under 5 kg (11 lb), small 5–10 kg (11–22 lb), medium 10–25 kg (22–55 lb), large 25–45 kg (55–99 lb) and giant 45 kg (99 lb) and over. More in dog size classes explained.

Airplane cabin labels

The ✈ labels assume the strictest common rule: dog and carrier together under 8 kg (17.6 lb), with a 1.5 kg carrier. "Fits" means the whole weight range of the breed is under 6.5 kg; "lighter dogs" means only part of the range is. See flying with a dog in the cabin.

Illustrations

Each breed is drawn in a typical coat and colour, scaled so that the withers sit at the breed's height. Heads, ears and tails rise above that line as they do on a real dog. The illustrations show proportions, not exact individual dogs.

Limitations

Breed standards describe the ideal show dog. Pet dogs, working lines and dogs from different countries can be noticeably bigger or smaller, and many individual dogs fall outside every range shown. For anything health-related, such as ideal weight, ask your vet.
