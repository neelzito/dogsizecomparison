How big is a German Shorthaired Pointer?

The German Shorthaired Pointer is a large-sized dog breed from Germany, in the Sporting group. Males stand 59–64 cm (23–25 in) at the shoulder and weigh 25–32 kg (55–71 lb). Females stand 54–59 cm (21.1–23 in) and weigh 21–27 kg (45–60 lb). On average it is taller than 67% of the 267 breeds on this site, and it is heavier than 66%. At the shoulder, an average male German Shorthaired Pointer reaches about mid-thigh on a 175 cm (5′9″) adult.

Typical life expectancy is 10–12 years, shorter than the 12.1-year average for large breeds.

About the German Shorthaired Pointer

The German Shorthaired Pointer was developed in Germany in the 19th century as a versatile hunting dog, one that could do everything: find and point game birds, retrieve on land and from water, track wounded game and even hunt rabbits and foxes. German breeders crossed older pointing dogs with English Pointers and other breeds to add speed and style. The result was one of the most capable all-round gundogs in the world. Today the GSP is a popular hunting companion and an energetic family dog for active homes.

What a GSP's size means at home

A male GSP stands about 58–64 cm at the shoulder, roughly an adult's mid-thigh, and weighs about 25–32 kg (55–70 lb). Females are about 53–59 cm and 20–27 kg. It is lean, muscular and athletic. It usually needs a 48″ (122 cm) crate (check with the crate size calculator) and a big boot.

GSP vs Vizsla and Wirehaired Vizsla

The GSP is a little taller and heavier than the Vizsla, and usually liver or liver-and-white, often ticked or roan, rather than golden-rust. The Wirehaired Vizsla is similar in size, with a wiry coat and a beard. The German Wirehaired Pointer is the GSP's rough-coated cousin.

Exercise: a lot of it

GSPs are among the most energetic of all breeds. They need at least one to two hours of hard exercise every day: running, swimming, retrieving, hunting or dog sports. They make excellent partners for runners and cyclists once fully grown. Without enough exercise and mental work, many become restless, noisy or destructive. Their hunting instinct is strong, so cats, poultry and wildlife may need protecting, and recall needs a lot of training.

Coat and care

The short, dense coat needs little more than a weekly brush, and it dries quickly after swimming. It sheds moderately all year. GSPs feel the cold more than long-coated dogs, so a coat helps on cold, wet days when they're not working.

Health

GSPs are generally healthy. Known problems include hip and elbow dysplasia, bloat (gastric dilatation-volvulus, as in other deep-chested breeds), some eye diseases, and a rare form of lupus (EFCLE) for which a DNA test exists. They often live 10–12 years.

Sources: AKC breed standard and breed history; FCI standard No. 119 (Deutsch Kurzhaar); The Kennel Club breed standard.

German Shorthaired Pointer size: common questions

How big does a German Shorthaired Pointer get?

A fully grown male German Shorthaired Pointer stands 59–64 cm (23–25 in) at the shoulder (withers) and weighs 25–32 kg (55–71 lb). Females stand 54–59 cm (21.1–23 in) and weigh 21–27 kg (45–60 lb).

Is a German Shorthaired Pointer a small, medium or large dog?

With an average weight of about 26 kg (58 lb), the German Shorthaired Pointer counts as a large dog breed on this site (large = 25–45 kg (55–99 lb) on average). It is taller than 67% of the 267 breeds listed.

How long does a German Shorthaired Pointer live?

Typical life expectancy is 10–12 years, shorter than the 12.1-year average for large breeds.

Can a German Shorthaired Pointer fly in the airplane cabin?

Usually not. A German Shorthaired Pointer weighs 21–32 kg (45–71 lb), well over the 8 kg (17.6 lb) cabin limit many airlines set for dog and carrier, and is too big for an under-seat carrier. Most airlines fly dogs this size in the hold (cargo).

Sizes are breed-standard ranges for adult dogs; individual dogs, working lines and dogs from different countries can be bigger or smaller. Airline notes are a general guide only. Check with your airline and your vet. Disclaimer.
